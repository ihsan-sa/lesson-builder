<Section title="Combining resistors">
  <P>
    Take a 4 Ω and a 12 Ω resistor and connect them side by side between the same two nodes. Their
    combination behaves as <M>{"\\frac{4 \\cdot 12}{4 + 12} = 3\\ \\Omega"}</M>, and a 6 Ω resistor added the
    same way brings the total down to <M>{"2\\ \\Omega"}</M>. Notice that the result is smaller than any of
    the three.
  </P>
  <P>
    Resistors connected between the same pair of nodes are said to be in parallel. Each sees the same
    voltage <M>{"V"}</M>, so the currents add: <M>{"I = V/R_1 + V/R_2"}</M>, and the pair behaves as a single
    resistance <M>{"R_p"}</M> with <M>{"1/R_p = 1/R_1 + 1/R_2"}</M>. Because every added branch adds another
    path for current at the same voltage, <M>{"R_p"}</M> is always smaller than the smallest branch.
  </P>
  <P>
    Resistors carrying one common current end to end are in series and simply add:
    <M>{"R_s = R_1 + R_2"}</M>, because the same current produces both voltage drops and the drops add.
  </P>
</Section>
